# Onboarding voice: the rules and where they come from

Looked up on 19 September 2026, when Peter called the walk's words «fluff» and «tonedeaf» and named the publishers to read (`docs/FEED-IA.md` §268). The walk it serves: `Get started` hands straight to Meta's WhatsApp connection with one button. The shop types its number on Meta's own screen, because Meta documents no field that carries a number into the WhatsApp Business app connection (`WIRING-SPEC.md` §2). [ONBOARDING-SPEC.md](ONBOARDING-SPEC.md), «The voice of the walk», holds the short form of these rules.

Every quotation below was read by a researcher on the page or image linked. Nothing is paraphrased into quotation marks.

## 1. The rules

**1. Title the first screen with the task the person is about to do or with the product's own name, never with the brand wrapped around the person's own things.**
- "Don't advertise. The launch screen isn't a branding opportunity. Avoid creating a screen that looks like a splash screen or an 'About' window, and don't include logos or other branding elements unless they're a fixed part of your app's first screen." — [Apple, HIG: Launching](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/launching)
- "Do: Get started with Uber / Don't: It's easy to drive with Uber" — [Uber Base, Writing for users](https://base.uber.com/6d2425e9f/v/56139/p/03856f-writing-for-users)
- "Do: Online store dashboard / Custom reports / Sell your products in person. Don't: This is your online store dashboard / Custom Reports / Sell your products in person!" — [Shopify Polaris, Actionable language](https://web.archive.org/web/2023/https://polaris.shopify.com/content/actionable-language)

**2. Cut "your" and "my" out of titles and row labels; the bare noun carries the same meaning in fewer words.**
- "Use possessive pronouns sparingly. Possessive pronouns like my and your are often unnecessary to establish context. For example, 'Favorites' conveys the same message as 'Your Favorites,' and is more succinct." — [Apple, HIG: Writing](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/writing)

**3. Label a row by what the person gets (a noun phrase) or by what the person does (an imperative verb), using the most obvious existing word for it.**
- "For a feature like this, a verb works well because we're putting the person using it in control. The feature is something you do, not something you have." — [Apple, WWDC26 session 290](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2026/290/)
- "When you're building something, it's natural to name it by what it does, the technology behind it, or the function. But the people using your app don't see it that way. They want to know what it does for them." — [Apple, WWDC26 session 290](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2026/290/)
- "It's the industry-standard term for it because it's well-understood, clear, and neutral. Clarity and trust come first here, not brand expression. […] Sometimes the most obvious word is the right one because it's already doing the job." — [Apple, WWDC26 session 290](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2026/290/)

**4. Under the row label goes exactly one sentence, and it adds what the label does not already say.**
- "The heading 'Market Data' is clear and simple and tells you exactly what the app is about. The next line, 'View stock quotes, interactive charts, and other financial metrics.' is helpful because it provides more detail about what Market Data you'll have access to." — [Apple, WWDC24 session 10140](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2024/10140/)
- "Avoid repetition. Repetitive language in your app is also a form of filler. Resist the urge to fill empty space by saying the same thing in different ways." — [Apple, WWDC25 session 404](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/404/)

**5. Put the reason in front of the instruction.**
- "All I need to do is take the second part of the sentence, 'to get reservation updates', and move it to the front. 'To get reservation updates, enter your phone number.' Now, the benefit of getting those updates is right at the front of this sentence, where it's easiest to see and has the most impact." — [Apple, WWDC25 session 404](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/404/)
- "Start headers and body text with information that corresponds with user goals, not to the action that's required in the UI. Do: To arrive on time, request a ride within 15 minutes. / Don't: Request a ride within 15 min to arrive on time." — [Uber Base, Writing for users](https://base.uber.com/6d2425e9f/v/56139/p/03856f-writing-for-users)

**6. A button is one verb plus its object, and the flow words are fixed — one to open, one to advance, one to end.**
- "To use text, write a few words that succinctly describe what the button does. Using title-style capitalization, consider starting the label with a verb to help convey the button's action — for example, a button that lets people add items to their shopping cart might use the label 'Add to Cart.'" — [Apple, HIG: Buttons](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/buttons)
- "Begin with language like 'Get Started' to indicate you're starting a flow. You can use the button label to hint at the next step, or use terms like 'Continue' or 'Next,' but be consistent with what you choose. Make it clear when a flow is complete by using language like 'Done.'" — [Apple, HIG: Writing](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/writing)
- "use the {verb} + {noun} content formula on buttons except in the case of common actions like 'Done,' 'Close,' 'Cancel,' or 'OK.' Do: Activate Apple Pay / Explore free themes / View shipping settings. Don't: Try Apple Pay / Free themes / Settings." — [Shopify Polaris, Actionable language](https://web.archive.org/web/2023/https://polaris.shopify.com/content/actionable-language)
- "In button labels, use an ampersand between 2 verbs. Note that CTA guidelines recommend against using 2 verbs in most cases." — [Uber Base, Punctuation](https://base.uber.com/6d2425e9f/v/56139/p/753d46-punctuation)

**7. Sentence case on everything — titles, row labels, buttons — and a full stop only on body sentences.**
- "We use sentence case, with few exceptions. It's conversational, easy to read, and takes up less space than other styles. […] This guidance applies to headers, subheads, button labels, and everything else." — [Uber Base, Capitalization](https://base.uber.com/6d2425e9f/v/56139/p/471fac-capitalization)
- "All text, including titles, headings, labels, menu items, navigation components, app bars, and buttons should use sentence-style capitalization." — [Material Design 3, UX writing best practices](https://m3.material.io/foundations/content-design/style-guide/ux-writing-best-practices)
- "Use punctuation at the end of a sentence in body text below a headline. Omit periods in single-sentence tooltips, ghost text, toasts, list items, and other strings that stand alone." — [Uber Base, Punctuation](https://base.uber.com/6d2425e9f/v/56139/p/753d46-punctuation)

**8. Write to the person as "you", let the product act in the third person, keep first person singular for consent, and never write "we".**
- "Write in the second-person voice (you, your) when you compose explanatory or guiding text, for elements such as titles and descriptions. […] Use the first-person singular voice whenever a user needs to grant consent or accept legal terms and conditions. […] Use this CTA: I accept / I agree / Review my documents — Don't use this CTA: Accept / You agree / Review your documents" — [Uber Base, Product tone](https://base.uber.com/6d2425e9f/v/56139/p/136623-product-tone)
- "Avoid using we altogether because it may be unclear who the 'we' in question refers to. This is particularly problematic in error messages like 'We're having trouble loading this content.' Something like 'Unable to load content' is much clearer." — [Apple, HIG: Writing](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/writing)
- "we Don't use first person; rewrite in terms of the reader or the product." — [Apple Style Guide](https://help.apple.com/pdf/applestyleguide/en_US/apple-style-guide.pdf)

**9. Length is the fewest words that carry the facts: one idea per screen, sentences under 25 words, and no adjective, adverb or interjection that carries none.**
- "Keep chunks of text short. Paragraphs should have no more than 5 sentences each. Try to split up sentences that are over 25 words long." — [GOV.UK, Clear language](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/writing-guidelines/clear-language/)
- "Use the fewest words necessary to communicate clearly. Make sure every word has a purpose; if it doesn't, cut it." / "Avoid using adjectives like great, easy, helpful, simple, best, amazing, friendly, and so on. Avoid using adverbs like always, never, quickly, simply, and so on." — [Uber Base, Writing for users](https://base.uber.com/6d2425e9f/v/56139/p/03856f-writing-for-users)
- "Interjections like 'oops!' or 'uh-oh' are typically unnecessary and can sound insincere." — [Apple, HIG: Writing](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/writing)
- "please Avoid using please in instructional text and cross-references." — [Apple Style Guide](https://help.apple.com/pdf/applestyleguide/en_US/apple-style-guide.pdf)

**10. Ask for access only where the product cannot work without it, and write the reason as one active, specific sentence in sentence case ending in a full stop.**
- "Request access only to data that you actually need. Asking for more data than a feature needs — or asking for data before a person shows interest in the feature — can make it hard for people to trust your app." — [Apple, HIG: Privacy](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/privacy)
- "If your app or game needs access to private data or resources before it can function, consider integrating the permission request into your onboarding flow. In this scenario, making the request during your onboarding flow gives you the opportunity to show people why your app or game needs their permission and the benefits of granting it." — [Apple, HIG: Onboarding](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/onboarding)
- "Aim for a brief, complete sentence that's straightforward, specific, and easy to understand. Use sentence case, avoid passive voice, and include a period at the end." — [Apple, HIG: Privacy](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/privacy)

**11. The screen that hands someone into the next system carries one button titled Continue or Next, and it hands over the data rather than instructions about where to type it.**
- "Include only one button and make it clear that it opens the system alert. […] Use a term like 'Continue' or 'Next' to title the single button in your custom screen or window, clarifying that its action is to open the system alert. […] Don't include an option to cancel. Don't include an option to close the view." — [Apple, HIG: Privacy](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/privacy)
- "Another type of manipulation is using a term like 'Allow' to title the custom screen's button. If the custom button seems similar in meaning and visual weight to the allow button in the alert, people can be more likely to choose the alert's allow button without meaning to." — [Apple, HIG: Privacy](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/privacy)
- "If you need to direct someone to a setting, provide a direct link or button, rather than trying to describe its location." — [Apple, HIG: Writing](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/writing)

**12. A screen built from inference states the observable fact it stands on, leads with the most likely row, shows nothing below a confidence threshold, and keeps a correction one tap away.**
- "Keep attributions factual and based on objective analysis. […] Don't provide an attribution that implies understanding or judgment of people's emotions, preferences, or beliefs. For example, an app that recommends new content to people can use an attribution like 'Because you've read nonfiction' instead of an attribution like 'Because you love nonfiction.'" — [Apple, HIG: Machine learning](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/machine-learning)
- "List the most likely option first. […] For suggestions and proactive features, it's a good idea to set a confidence threshold below which you don't offer results." / "In general, avoid technical or statistical jargon. In most situations, using percentages, statistics, and other technical jargon doesn't help people assess the results you provide." / "Make it easy for people to correct frequent or predictable mistakes. If you don't give people an easy way to correct mistakes, they can lose trust in your app." — [Apple, HIG: Machine learning](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/machine-learning)
- "Set clear expectations about what your AI-powered feature can and can't do. […] If your feature has known limitations, let people know up front, show them how to get good results, and explain why inferior results occur." — [Apple, HIG: Generative AI](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/generative-ai)

**13. An invitation says what each person does, in order, and states the reward as the condition it actually is.**
- "Invite your friends to Monzo and you could both get a referral bonus." / "Step 1: Click the button above to get your unique refer a friend link. Share it with your friend. Step 2: Your friend accepts the invite and applies for a Monzo account (within 30 days). Step 3: Your friend makes their first card payment (within 30 days)." — [Monzo, Refer a friend](https://monzo.com/i/refer-a-friend/)
- "Don't: Assume the next step or outcome is guaranteed. You're just a few steps away from receiving your first order. […] Don't: Oversell or overpromise." — [Shopify Polaris, Voice and tone](https://web.archive.org/web/2023/https://polaris.shopify.com/content/voice-and-tone)

**14. An error or a waiting state names the specific thing that happened plus the one way forward, and an inline error takes no end punctuation.**
- "Never blame the user, even if an error is their fault. Don't use Something went wrong; be more specific and therefore reassuring. Always provide a way forward, even if it's just to say Try again. […] Oops | Never use | It sets the wrong tone and trivializes issues that matter to the user. […] Don't use end punctuation: Card expired / Enter a 9-digit SSN / The passwords don't match" — [Uber Base, Messages](https://base.uber.com/6d2425e9f/v/56139/p/000786-messages)
- "Above all else, be clear — your writing should help our users accomplish their goals. Do: Arriving within 5 minutes / Don't: Arriving soon. Do: Your photo was blurry. Try taking a new one with your face fully in the frame. / Don't: There was a problem with your photo. Try taking a new one." — [Uber Base, Writing for users](https://base.uber.com/6d2425e9f/v/56139/p/03856f-writing-for-users)

## 3. What they never do

- Put the brand or a logo in a setup screen's title — Apple, HIG: Launching ("The launch screen isn't a branding opportunity").
- Label anything "Your X" where the noun alone works — Apple, HIG: Writing (possessive pronouns).
- Write "we" — Apple, HIG: Writing and the Apple Style Guide entry for *we*.
- Use adjectives or adverbs of ease (great, easy, simple, quickly, simply) — Uber Base, Writing for users.
- Use "please", "sorry", "oops", or an exclamation mark as filler — Apple Style Guide (*please*), Apple HIG: Writing (interjections), Uber Base, Messages ("Oops | Never use").
- Repeat the heading in the body — Apple, WWDC25 session 404 ("Avoid repetition").
- Run an em dash into a complete sentence, i.e. slogan cadence — Uber Base, Punctuation ("Don't use an em dash to introduce a complete sentence").
- Put two verbs, a bare Yes/No, or a clever line in a button — Uber Base, Punctuation; Apple HIG: Writing ("just saying 'Send' often works better than 'Let's do it!'").
- Title a hand-off button "Allow", or give that screen a second button — Apple, HIG: Privacy (pre-alert screens).
- Describe where a setting lives instead of giving the button — Apple, HIG: Writing (keep settings labels clear and simple).
- Spell out what happens when something is off — Apple, HIG: Writing ("Describe what it does when turned on, and people can infer the opposite").
- Ask for data before the person has shown interest in the feature that needs it — Apple, HIG: Privacy.
- Teach the person how their phone or another company's app works inside onboarding — Apple, HIG: Onboarding ("they don't need to learn how to use the system or the device").
- Celebrate a setup step, call a completed step a success, or promise the outcome — Shopify Polaris, Voice and tone.
- Say "every", "all" or "most" about results — Shopify Polaris, Voice and tone.
- Show a percentage or an emotional attribution on an inferred row — Apple, HIG: Machine learning.
- Write "Something went wrong" — Uber Base, Messages.
- Name the UI or use a directional word ("the button below") — Uber Base, Writing for users.
- Capitalise ordinary words to manufacture a feature name — Uber Base, Writing for users.

## 4. How this meets the house rules

1. **Say what IS** has two publishers behind it, not just the house: Uber prints it as a Do/Don't ("Use positive words and sentiments, instead of negative ones, to explain a situation. Do: Your account needs attention / Don't: Something's wrong with your account", [Writing for users](https://base.uber.com/6d2425e9f/v/56139/p/03856f-writing-for-users)) and GOV.UK gives the reading evidence ("avoid negative contractions like 'can't' or 'don't'. Many users find negative contractions hard to read, or misread them as the opposite of what they say", [Clear language](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/writing-guidelines/clear-language/)).
2. **A heading names the thing blatantly** is Polaris's rule verbatim ("Online store dashboard", never "This is your online store dashboard"), GOV.UK's stand-alone title test ("Your title should make sense: by itself", [Clear titles](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/writing-guidelines/clear-titles/)) and Apple's possessive cut, all pointing the same way; the self-comprehension test and rule 2 above delete the same words.
3. **No slogan cadence, no kicker**: Uber bans the construction mechanically (no em dash introducing a complete sentence), Apple bans the register ("Send" over "Let's do it!"), and in all fourteen screens read today no publisher ships an eyebrow line above a title — the title carries the message or it is cut.
4. **The one real tension** is verb-led task titles versus noun labels for sections, and the sources resolve it by surface rather than by taste: WWDC26 decides each label by what it is ("The feature is something you do, not something you have"), every publisher titles a *step* with the task ("Add a phone number for WhatsApp", "Enter your mobile number", "Elige un tipo de cuenta", "Transfer from Android"), and a *section of a page* keeps the noun ("Online store dashboard", "Market Data"); so the house noun law governs sections of a page and the verb governs a step in a flow, and a phone-first screen is a step.
5. **Two smaller frictions, both already resolved by the sources**: English title case does not port to Peruvian Spanish, and Uber plus Material already demand sentence case everywhere, so drafting the English strings in sentence case removes the problem before translation (structure ports, capitalisation does not); and saffron-for-"Rocoto acted" agrees with Apple's machine-learning rule, since the colour marks an action the product took while the row still states the observable fact — "Because you've read nonfiction", never "Because you love nonfiction", and never a percentage. Where Meta's own screens lean on denials ("This doesn't let [Provider] post to Facebook"), that denial belongs to Meta's screen; ours says what IS.
